Choosing a typeface pairing for a terminal site

The chrome speaks in monospace; the prose needs a different voice. Same page, four candidate serifs.

Everything you see above and below this paragraph — the breadcrumb, the $ identify block, the ledger rows — is set in the mono face. This paragraph, and everything inside the article, is set in the candidate serif. Read a few paragraphs here: if your eyes relax, the pairing works; if you keep noticing the face, it doesn't.

The test for an editorial face inside a terminal aesthetic is quiet contrast: the serif should feel like paper laid inside the machine, not a second machine. Weight matters more than family — at 1.18rem with a 1.65 line height, most OFL serifs become readable; the difference shows up in the italics, the dark-background rendering, and how the x-height sits against the mono's taller one.

The mono is the machine's voice. The serif is yours. They share a page without sharing a tone — that's the whole pairing argument.

Check the italics in this sentence and the summary line above — Soliloquy and Studies lean on italic summaries, so a serif with a lazy or cramped italic fails here even if its roman is fine. Then check inline code and numbers: 1,240 requests, 42 files, 0.82rem — the serif and mono digits should not fight.

Round two: the serif bar now also carries sans candidates for the editorial voice, and the mono bar carries the fun round — Space Mono (retro-quirky), Monaspace Radon (handwriting-flavored mono) and Xenon (serif-mono), Kode, Martian (wide, chunky), VT323 and Departure (pixel terminals), Comic Mono. Fun faces are titlebar/accent candidates — judge whether the chrome stays readable with them, not whether you'd read a book in them.
