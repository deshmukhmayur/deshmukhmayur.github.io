The quiet texture of long pages

A serif faces its real test not in a specimen sheet but in the fifteenth paragraph, where every kerning wobble compounds into a reading texture you can feel.

Most type decisions are made in the first screenful, but the honest verdict arrives much later. A face that looks lively at one hundred words can turn fussy at one thousand. The serifs you admired in the headline start to flicker at the edges of your attention, and you find yourself reading the font instead of the sentence.

What makes a body face calm is not the absence of personality — it's the repetition of it. When the letterforms repeat evenly, the eye stops counting them. The words become a texture, like fabric seen from across the room: you notice the weave only when you lean in. Good prose type is the weave, and the argument is the room.

Where the details live

The details that matter at reading size are small ones: the terminal of an italic 'e', whether figures share the lowercase height, how tightly the space after a period collapses. At 16–19px, a serif that was drawn for print at 9pt behaves differently than one tuned for a screen — hints rasterize, counters fill, and thin strokes drop below the pixel grid.

"The reader should not notice the type. If they notice it, it has already begun to compete with the text."

That competition is subtle. It shows up as a low-grade fatigue: you reach the bottom of a long page and feel like you read it, rather than having moved through it. The fix is rarely a redesign — it's usually a change of font-family, a nudge to line-height, a slightly wider measure.

So the test is simple and unglamorous: read this whole page twice, once in each face, and notice where your eye snagged. The winner is the one you forget.
